## Course Description

This course considers the nature and contemporary forms of digital culture. Broadly speaking, this can be defined as the diverse range of symbolic practices through which communities affirm and maintain their cultural identities using digital media devices and interfaces in a globally networked society. While these practices are structured by deeply unequal power relations, are contradictory, and often come into conflict with one another, collectively they constitute what may be considered a global digital culture.

A key component of the course is the automation of various forms of creative production, from writing to the visual arts, by natural-language processing computational systems (generically known as "AI"). The course addresses some of the many issues raised by such systems, with a particular focus on questions of aesthetics and the increasingly contested relationship between artists and algorithms. While such systems now demonstrably pass the Turing Test (i.e. pass as human or their products as human-produced), they also compel us to reconsider what we mean by “art,” or “intelligence” itself.


## Curriculum objectives

This course is in fulfilment of the Interdisciplinary Perspective portion of the General Education Curriculum. It seeks to challenge students to understand and appraise the role of interdisciplinary knowledge in the arts, culture, and/or human affairs by exploring how at least two disciplinary approaches can be brought together to address a topic in a given area.

## Format

This is a critical-thinking course, which means that it encourages you to think reflexively and analytically about the digitally-mediated cultural practices that the course considers, as well as to participate in them; for example, you will be invited to experiment with image-synthesis and text-generating software and analyze the results using key concepts and theoretical frameworks.

## Course-specific learning objectives

By the end of the course, students will:

- have acquired a deeper understanding of the social, cultural, and political dimensions of digital technologies and networked communication;
- be able to apply critical thinking to contemporary developments in digital culture using relevant analytical concepts and both qualitative and quantitative methodologies such as cultural analytics;
- understand basic principles of algorithmic image synthesis on a variety of platforms;
- have reflected upon and discussed the larger significance of machine learning systems within global networked societies.

## Course requirements

**Participation and attendance** (10%)

It is important that you come to class prepared with the readings read, digested, in short, ready to actively discuss. This portion of your grade is based both on active participation in class, and partially on attendance (see below on attendance policy). 

You should arm yourself with questions about things that made you curious from them or things that bewildered you. Such discussion is the only way to make sense of many of the concepts that we will be encountering. 

Please communicate with me if you expect to miss class. Missing in-class activities (see below) will be detrimental to your grade, but if you let me know ahead of time you'll be absent, you will not be penalized.

**Reflections** (30%)

For each class, please bring with you one or two quotations from the readings that you feel provide the "core" of what the author(s) are trying to express. It would also be a good idea to have in mind some current event that has transpired. 

**Midterm and Final examinations** (20% + 20%)

There will be two examinations/evaluations in the course of the semester to synthesize the material from the course.

**Research Paper/Project** (20%) 

Research on an approved topic relevant to the course. Individual or group. Further details will be provided after Spring Break. 1,250-1,500 words.

## Policies


### Missed classes

We are all adults, so rather than taking attendance on a regular basis, I prefer a different mode of gauging your presence. As mentioned above, I will often have class activities, brief writing exercises, and randomly-taken sign-ins. If you are present for these activities, you will receive points toward the "Reflections and in-class activities" portion of your grade (note that this is something quite aside from your 'Participation' grade). If you are not, and you have given me no advance notice of a legitimate reason for your absence, you will receive no points, and your ultimate score will reflect this.

### Zoom and attendance

Different classes and professors have differing policies for Zoom attendance in class. Ours is a discussion-oriented class, which will involve in-class activities and group work at points. For this reason, remote attendance is generally not feasible; it both renders these activities difficult, if not impossible, for the remote participant; similarly, having unexpected remote participants requires big shifts in how classes are run. Thus, if you expect to miss class, please let me know, and I can gauge whether or not it is feasible to zoom in.

### Laptops

Please bring your laptops to every class, but in general, we'll have them and your phones put away. This is a discussion-oriented class. Toward that end, the research is starting to pile up that laptop use is thoroughly distracting to others and hurts the classroom environment. Additionally, notetaking is much more effective with pen-and-paper, as you're forced to distill what is going on in the class that much more effectively. Thus, when we meet in person, please be sure to bring your laptop to class, as we may use it for any variety of activities and reflections, but in general, they must be put away unless you are looking up something related to our classroom discussion. Cell phones must be on silent or vibrate and should be put away for the duration of class. There will be no exceptions. Cell phones being used in class will result in them being collected for retrieval at the end of the class session.

### AI

Much like any tool, artificial intelligence has its place in particular workflows, and it is an emerging feature of our world, albeit one that is still forming. Its final form, and the ways we'll choose (or have chosen for us) to rearrange our labor, institutions, even societies around it remain to be seen. Dominant players are seeking to obtain and lock-in their own advantage. Unlike a calculator, however, to which I've heard the technology compared, a key facet of it is that it is a technology that its use changes the world in material ways. Anything you enter into an AI engine, your ideas, your valuable information, more, is stored and integrated forever. We're presently in the 'learning' phase of these technologies, and make no mistake, they are absolutely staving for your data, and their backers will make their use easy and cheap to encourage you to use them. With the understanding that elementary, middle, and high schools are all starting to teach students new workflows that utilize AI, it means all of us are thinking deeply about the pedagogical intent of our assignments and redesigning them accordingly. For this reason, for some assignments, I will absolutely restrict the use of AI for pedagogical reasons. There are other moments where using AI as an idea generation tool or timesaver can be more appropriate. Whether it is or not depends on the circumstance:

- For this reason, when we do any form of written reflections on the readings or other materials, AI use is absolutely forbidden unless noted otherwise. I want your unvarnished impressions, because only by working through theory do we really learn to use it as the tool that it is. Any use of AI on the reading reflections will result in a failing grade on them.
- I want your submitted work to be your own thoughts. AI can be a (sometimes!) useful summarizer and generator of ideas, which is fine, but your work-process and control over ideas needs to be your own. If you're in doubt about the appropriate use of a tool, ask, we're all in a position of learning as we go right now!
- Sometimes using AI image generators is a quick and dirty way of getting something done; other times, it's more useful to hunt for a 'real' image out in the wild, while they still exist: one is a creative tool, the other is actual research, although I can imagine ways in which the former can become the latter in certain contexts. Once again: if you're in doubt about the use of a tool, ask!

The Office of International Student Affairs (OISA) offers English language support services (https://emerson.edu/departments/international-student-affairs/english-language-learning) as well as immigration advising to assist international students in maintaining their required full-time registration (https://emerson.edu/departments/international-student-affairs/immigration-status/maintaining-your-f-1-status/academic-registration). Students needing English language assistance or immigration accommodations (including an authorized reduced course load for academic or medical reasons in their final term) should contact OISA staff by email at oisa@emerson.edu or by appointment, (https://emerson.edu/departments/international-student-affairs/contact-us) or may submit a reduced course load request via Terra Dotta (http://td.emerson.edu/).
